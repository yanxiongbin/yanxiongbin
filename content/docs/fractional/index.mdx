## Introduction

Anomalous diffusion arises in many complex systems where the spreading behavior 
of particles, energy, or signals deviates from the classical Brownian motion 
described by the standard diffusion equation. In contrast to normal diffusion, 
anomalous diffusion often exhibits **memory effects**, **nonlocal interactions**, 
and **heterogeneous transport behavior**, making it an important topic in applied 
mathematics, physics, engineering, and biological sciences.

Such phenomena appear in a wide range of applications, including transport in porous media, 
underground water flow, viscoelastic materials, biological cell migration, financial dynamics, 
and diffusion processes in complex disordered environments. In many of these settings, 
the observed transport is either slower or faster than classical diffusion, leading to 
the notions of **subdiffusion** and **superdiffusion**.

From a mathematical perspective, anomalous diffusion is often modeled by **fractional partial differential equations**, 
in which classical integer-order derivatives are replaced by fractional-order operators in time or space. 
These models provide an effective framework for describing long-range dependence, hereditary effects, and spatial nonlocality.

## Mathematical Models

A typical time-fractional diffusion equation can be written as

$$
\partial_t^\alpha u(x,t) - \Delta u(x,t) + p(x)u(x,t) = f(x,t),
\qquad 0<\alpha<1,
$$

where $\partial_t^\alpha$ denotes a fractional derivative in time, 
$u(x,t)$ is the state variable, $p(x)$ is an unknown or prescribed coefficient, and $f(x,t)$ is a source term.

More generally, anomalous diffusion models may involve

- **time-fractional derivatives**, which describe memory effects;
- **space-fractional operators**, which capture long-range jumps and nonlocal transport;
- **fractional diffusion-wave equations**, which interpolate between diffusion and wave propagation;
- **distributed-order models**, which describe multiple transport regimes.

These models have attracted significant attention in both theoretical analysis and numerical computation.

## Inverse Problems in Anomalous Diffusion

Inverse problems in anomalous diffusion aim to recover unknown quantities in the governing model from indirect observations. 
Depending on the application, one may seek to reconstruct

- the fractional order $ \alpha$,
- unknown coefficients or potentials,
- source terms,
- initial conditions,
- boundary conditions,
- diffusion parameters or kernels.

Such inverse problems are important because the physically relevant quantities are often not directly measurable. 
Instead, one only has access to partial observations of the system, such as boundary measurements, interior data, 
or time-series observations.

Mathematically, these problems are often **ill-posed**, meaning that solutions may fail to exist, 
may not be unique, or may depend unstably on the data. This makes inverse problems in anomalous 
diffusion both mathematically challenging and practically significant.

## Main Challenges

Inverse problems for anomalous diffusion involve several major difficulties.

First, the underlying forward models are already nonlocal and history-dependent, 
which makes both analysis and numerical simulation more complicated than in the classical diffusion case.

Second, the presence of fractional derivatives introduces strong memory effects, 
meaning that the solution depends on the entire past evolution of the system. 
This increases computational cost and complicates parameter reconstruction.

Third, inverse problems are typically highly sensitive to noise and incomplete data. 
In practice, one usually has only limited observations, which further amplifies the instability of the reconstruction.

Finally, when multiple unknowns are present, such as the simultaneous recovery of a 
fractional order and a spatial coefficient, the inverse problem becomes even more nonlinear and difficult.

## Research Interests

My research interests in this area include

- inverse problems for time-fractional and space-fractional diffusion equations,
- simultaneous reconstruction of fractional orders and model coefficients,
- mathematical analysis of uniqueness and stability,
- numerical methods for fractional inverse problems,
- deep-learning-based and physics-informed approaches for anomalous diffusion.

In particular, I am interested in combining mathematical analysis, scientific computing, 
and machine learning to develop effective methods for solving challenging inverse problems 
arising from anomalous diffusion models.

## Outlook

Anomalous diffusion provides a rich mathematical framework for modeling 
complex transport processes beyond the classical diffusion paradigm. 
The associated inverse problems connect fractional calculus, 
partial differential equations, inverse theory, and computational mathematics. 
They also offer many opportunities for interaction with modern data-driven methods 
and artificial intelligence, making this a promising research direction 
at the intersection of mathematics and scientific machine learning.
