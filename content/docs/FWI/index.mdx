## Introduction

Full-waveform inversion (FWI) is a powerful computational imaging technique used 
to recover physical parameters of a medium from recorded wavefield data. 
It plays an important role in geophysics, seismic exploration, medical imaging, 
and other areas involving wave propagation.

Unlike traditional imaging methods that rely on simplified approximations, 
FWI utilizes the **entire recorded waveform information**, including amplitude, 
phase, and multiple scattering effects. This allows it to produce high-resolution reconstructions of subsurface structures.

Mathematically, FWI is formulated as a **nonlinear inverse problem constrained 
by partial differential equations**, where one seeks to infer unknown model parameters 
by minimizing the mismatch between observed data and simulated wavefields generated by a forward physical model.



## Mathematical Formulation

Let $m$ denote the unknown model parameter (for example, the subsurface velocity field), 
and let $d^{\mathrm{obs}}$ represent the observed wavefield data. 
The forward wave propagation process can be written as
$$
\mathcal{F}(m)=d.
$$

Here $\mathcal{F}$ denotes the nonlinear forward operator defined by the underlying wave equation.

The inverse problem consists of recovering the unknown parameter $m$ from the observed data $d^{\mathrm{obs}}$. 
In practice, this problem is typically formulated as the following optimization problem:

$$
\min_m \; \frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{F}(m)-d^{\mathrm{obs}}\|^2+\mathcal{R}(m).
$$

Here $\mathcal{R}(m)$ is a regularization term introduced to stabilize the inversion and incorporate prior information about the model.

Solving this optimization problem generally requires repeated numerical simulations 
of the forward wave equation together with gradient computations obtained through the adjoint-state method.

## Challenges in Full-Waveform Inversion

Despite its high resolution and strong imaging capability, 
full-waveform inversion remains a challenging computational problem in practice.

First, FWI is a highly **nonlinear** inverse problem. The mapping from 
the model parameter to the observed wavefield is nonlinear, which makes 
the objective function complicated and often nonconvex. As a result, standard optimization methods may converge to undesirable local minima.

Second, the problem is typically **ill-posed**. Small perturbations in the 
observed data, such as measurement noise or modeling errors, may lead to 
large variations in the reconstructed model. This instability makes regularization 
and prior information essential in practical inversion.

Third, FWI is computationally very **expensive**. Each iteration of the 
inversion procedure usually requires repeated numerical solutions of large-scale wave 
propagation equations, together with adjoint-based gradient computations. 
For realistic high-dimensional applications, this leads to substantial computational and memory costs.

Another major difficulty is the well-known issue of **cycle skipping**. 
If the initial model is not sufficiently close to the true one, the simulated 
and observed waveforms may differ by more than half a wavelength, causing the 
inversion to converge to an incorrect solution.

In addition, practical applications often involve **incomplete, noisy, 
and limited-aperture data**, as well as uncertainties in the forward physical model. 
These factors further increase the difficulty of obtaining stable and accurate reconstructions.

Because of these challenges, the design of robust, efficient, 
and scalable algorithms for FWI remains an active research topic in inverse problems, 
scientific computing, and computational mathematics.
